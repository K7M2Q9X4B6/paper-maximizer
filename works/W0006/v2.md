# A missing observation interval changes my volcanic-risk estimate

## Answer and correction

My candidate for an underrated risk remains a large sulfur-rich volcanic eruption and the food-system disruption it could initiate. My revised working estimate for at least one qualifying eruption during 2027–2100 is **about 11%**, conditional on using the source's frequency reconstruction and a stationary Poisson model.

This is a probability of the initiating eruption, not of global famine, permanent civilizational collapse, or human extinction. I still cannot defend a numerical probability for those downstream outcomes from the evidence checked here. The judgment that this risk is underrated remains my assessment, not a measured ranking of public beliefs.

This version corrects an error I made in W0006@1. I divided the reported event count by the full calendar span of the study. The paper's frequency analysis excludes an interval without identified bipolar eruptions. Its observation exposure is therefore shorter. The previous arithmetic passed because the check asserted the calculation I had written; it did not check whether I had selected the right denominator.

## What the source actually uses

[Lin et al. (2022), section 4.3, *Climate of the Past*, doi:10.5194/cp-18-485-2022](https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/18/485/2022/), explicitly subtract the 24.5–16.5 ka interval from the 60–9 ka span when discussing bipolar eruption frequencies. Section 3.3 explains the synchronization difficulty. Section 4.3 reports a larger-than-Tambora reconstructed forcing rate of approximately 1.60 events per millennium.

I now follow that exposure convention. It does not recover events during the missing interval or establish that missingness was random. It also does not make reconstructed forcing, sulfur emissions, and directly measured eruption magnitude interchangeable.

```check
import math
from fractions import Fraction as F

calendar_span = (60 - 9) * 1000
missing_interval = F(24500) - F(16500)
exposure = F(calendar_span) - missing_interval
events = 69
years = 2101 - 2027

assert calendar_span == 51000
assert missing_interval == 8000
assert exposure == 43000
assert years == 74

corrected_rate = F(events) / exposure
old_rate = F(events, calendar_span)
assert 1.60 < float(corrected_rate * 1000) < 1.61

old_probability = -math.expm1(-float(old_rate) * years)
corrected_probability = -math.expm1(-float(corrected_rate) * years)
century_probability = -math.expm1(-float(corrected_rate) * 100)

assert 0.0952 < old_probability < 0.0954
assert 0.1119 < corrected_probability < 0.1121
assert 0.1482 < century_probability < 0.1483
assert round(100 * corrected_probability) == 11
assert corrected_probability > old_probability
```

Thus the specified remaining-century probability changes from about 9.5% to about 11.2%; a full-century extrapolation is about 14.8%. These are my model calculations, not forecasts independently validated by the Python assertions.

## Why missing events do not create a future lower bound

The review says that missed eruptions would raise the hazard estimate. That is true if all other assumptions are held fixed. It is not an unconditional lower bound on future risk.

To show the distinction, let c be historical detection completeness and g the future-to-historical rate multiplier. In a simplified model the future rate is the observed rate multiplied by g/c. A completeness correction raises the inferred historical rate, but an unknown change in future frequency can offset it. The following inputs are illustrative, not estimates from volcanic data.

```check
import math
from fractions import Fraction as F

observed_rate = F(69, 43000)
years = 74

def probability(completeness, future_multiplier):
    rate = observed_rate * future_multiplier / completeness
    return -math.expm1(-float(rate) * years)

baseline = probability(F(1), F(1))
missed_half_only = probability(F(1, 2), F(1))
missed_half_and_lower_future_rate = probability(F(1, 2), F(1, 4))

assert missed_half_only > baseline
assert missed_half_and_lower_future_rate < baseline
assert F(1, 4) / F(1, 2) == F(1, 2)
```

The review already identifies stationarity as a major uncertainty. The conditional wording above makes its missing-events discussion consistent with that warning. Neither this toy model nor the corrected exposure resolves nonstationarity.

## What could change my judgment

A reconstruction from genuinely additional observations, with comparable event thresholds and an explicit treatment of incomplete recording, could change the frequency estimate. A finding that the excluded interval has a systematically different event rate would also change how I interpret this extrapolation.

My concern about food systems needs separate consequence evidence: sulfur loading and eruption geography, climate response, crops, trade, reserves, and institutions. Demonstrating an eruption rate does not demonstrate a famine probability. Food-system studies showing reliable preservation of access under severe modeled shocks would weaken my underratedness judgment.
