# The underrated risk of a large volcanic eruption and a global food shock

## Answer and scope

My candidate for the most underrated risk is a large sulfur-rich volcanic eruption, especially its potential to disrupt food production across countries. "Most underrated" is my judgment about a neglected failure mode, not a measured ranking of public beliefs or an assertion that volcanoes are the largest existential risk.

**My working estimate is about 10% for at least one eruption exceeding Tambora's reconstructed sulfur-emission strength during 2027–2100.** This estimates the initiating hazard. It does not mean a 10% probability of global famine, permanent civilizational collapse, or human extinction. I cannot give a defensible numerical estimate for those outcomes from the evidence checked here. That distinction is essential to answering the broader question in W0002@1 honestly.

The forecast starts at the next complete calendar year after this report's date, 2026-10-25. It does not estimate the already elapsed part of this century.

## Source and reproducible estimate

[Lin et al. (2022), *Magnitude, frequency and climate forcing of global volcanism during the last glacial period as seen in Greenland and Antarctic ice cores*, doi:10.5194/cp-18-485-2022](https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/18/485/2022/), report 69 eruptions with estimated sulfur emissions exceeding Tambora's during their 60–9 thousand-years-before-2000 study interval (abstract and conclusions). This is a sulfate-based reconstruction, not a census of directly observed eruption volumes.

I use its event count and observation interval as a crude frequency estimate, assume a stationary Poisson process, and calculate the chance of at least one event in the specified forecast window. Those assumptions are mine; the following probability is my extrapolation, not a probability quoted from Lin et al.

```check
import math

events = 69
exposure_years = (60 - 9) * 1000
forecast_years = 2101 - 2027
rate = events / exposure_years
expected_events = rate * forecast_years
probability = -math.expm1(-expected_events)

assert exposure_years == 51000
assert forecast_years == 74
assert math.isclose(rate, 0.0013529411764705882, rel_tol=1e-12)
assert 0.095 < probability < 0.096
assert round(100 * probability) == 10
assert 0 <= probability <= expected_events
```

The result is about 9.5%, which I round to 10% to avoid false precision. A simple sensitivity check halves or doubles the assumed event rate; these are deliberately chosen stress tests, not a statistical confidence interval.

```check
import math

rate = 69 / 51000
years = 74
lower = -math.expm1(-rate * years / 2)
central = -math.expm1(-rate * years)
upper = -math.expm1(-rate * years * 2)

assert 0.048 < lower < 0.050
assert 0.181 < upper < 0.183
assert lower < central < upper
```

That gives roughly 5–18% under the stipulated rate changes. The interval captures neither all proxy uncertainty nor uncertainty about consequences.

There are good reasons to be cautious. [Wolff et al. (2023), *Frequency of large volcanic eruptions over the past 200 000 years*, doi:10.5194/cp-19-23-2023](https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/19/23/2023/), use a longer Antarctic record and discuss how transport, source latitude, missing peaks, and regional eruptions complicate inference (especially section 5). Its deposition thresholds are not interchangeable with a global Tambora-equivalent sulfur-emission threshold. I therefore do not pool the studies as independent samples or claim that changing the counting rule merely supplies another estimate of exactly the same event.

## Why this could be underrated

Volcanic danger is easy to picture as a local evacuation problem. My concern is a different exposure: an atmospheric disturbance can reach places far from the volcano, while food access also depends on storage, transport, trade, and political responses.

The physical starting point is supported by Wolff et al.'s introduction: sulfur dioxide reaching the stratosphere forms sulfate aerosol that affects climate. The rest of the chain needs separate evidence: climate response, crop losses, food distribution, and institutional resilience. My inference is that planning only for local damage can miss a common shock to food systems.

This argument does not establish that every large eruption produces a worldwide famine. Sulfur loading, latitude, season, atmospheric circulation, and societal responses matter. Nor does eruption magnitude by itself determine climate damage.

Here is a check of the logical limitation, not a forecast of famine. Let q be the unknown conditional probability of a specified severe food crisis given a qualifying eruption. In a simplified event tree where that crisis requires such an eruption, the joint probability is the eruption probability multiplied by q. The q values below are illustrative assumptions only.

```check
import math

p_eruption = -math.expm1(-(69 / 51000) * 74)
illustrative_q = (0.0, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0)
joint = [p_eruption * q for q in illustrative_q]

assert joint[0] == 0
assert math.isclose(joint[1], p_eruption / 10)
assert math.isclose(joint[2], p_eruption / 2)
assert joint[-1] == p_eruption
assert all(0 <= p <= p_eruption for p in joint)
```

Without estimating q, arithmetic cannot turn the hazard estimate into an estimate of societal collapse. I leave q unestimated rather than disguising an assumption as evidence.

## What would change my mind

One decisive piece of evidence would be an independently replicated, globally calibrated reconstruction showing that Tambora-equivalent sulfur-emission events occur much less often than the count used here, after accounting for missed events and geographic bias. That would lower my hazard estimate directly.

An ensemble coupling eruption physics, climate, crops, trade, and adaptive responses could separately change my judgment about neglected food-system consequences. Robust evidence that realistic responses preserve adequate food access across severe scenarios would make me downgrade this candidate even if eruption frequency stayed unchanged.

## What might reduce the risk

My proposed research and preparedness priorities are monitoring, rapid characterization of sulfur injection, and exercises that test food reserves and distribution under simultaneous regional harvest problems. I claim no quantified benefit for these proposals: that would require the consequence models missing above. These are general research priorities, not operational emergency guidance.
