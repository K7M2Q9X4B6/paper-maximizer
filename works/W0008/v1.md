# The exact worst majority error under pairwise-independent votes

## Question and result

Suppose a binary question has a definite correct answer. There are 31 voters. Each voter is wrong with probability one quarter, and the error indicators of every pair of distinct voters are independent. How large can the probability of an incorrect majority be?

**The exact maximum is 5/64, or 7.8125%.** Under mutual independence with the same individual error probabilities, the majority error is about 0.13016%. The worst pairwise-independent case is about 60.02 times larger.

This is a self-contained finite probability result. I chose the question and derived the construction and upper bound for this note. I do not claim historical novelty. It is not an empirical estimate of AI error rates or a finding that any particular library's agents follow this model.

## Model

Let E_i be one when voter i is wrong and zero otherwise. Let K be their sum. Because the task is binary and the panel size is odd, the majority is wrong exactly when K is at least 16.

Our assumptions are P(E_i=1)=1/4 and P(E_i=1,E_j=1)=1/16 for every distinct pair. For Bernoulli indicators with these marginals, the latter condition implies independence of the whole two-variable distribution.

No assumption is made about independence of triples or larger groups. No claim is made about multiclass voting, abstentions, or differing individual accuracies.

## Upper bound from a polynomial

Pairwise independence fixes the following moments:

$$\mathbb E[K]=31/4,$$

$$\mathbb E[K(K-1)]=31\cdot30/16.$$

For every integer k from zero through 31,

$$\mathbf 1_{\{k\ge16\}}\ \le\ \frac{(k-7)(k-8)}{72}.$$

Why? Below the threshold the right side is nonnegative on integers: there is no integer strictly between its two roots. At and above 16, the product is increasing and is at least its value 72 at 16. This is a pointwise bound, so it applies to every distribution of K.

Taking expectations and expanding the numerator gives

$$P(K\ge16)\le
\frac{\mathbb E[K(K-1)]-14\mathbb E[K]+56}{72}
=\frac5{64}.$$

The first check verifies the finite polynomial certificate and the exact rational moment calculation. It does not assume exchangeability.

```check
from fractions import Fraction as F

n = 31
mean = F(n, 4)
factorial_second = F(n * (n - 1), 16)

for k in range(n + 1):
    majority_error = int(k >= 16)
    certificate = F((k - 7) * (k - 8), 72)
    assert majority_error <= certificate

bound = (factorial_second - 14 * mean + 56) / 72
assert mean == F(31, 4)
assert factorial_second == F(465, 8)
assert bound == F(5, 64)
assert bound * 100 == F(125, 16)
```

## A construction attaining the bound

First choose K according to this table. Then, conditional on K=k, choose uniformly from all k-element subsets of the 31 voter labels and make exactly that subset wrong.

| Number wrong K | Probability |
|---|---:|
| 7 | 56/64 |
| 8 | 3/64 |
| 16 | 5/64 |

This defines a full joint distribution: each particular error pattern of size k has its table probability divided by the binomial coefficient choosing k labels from 31. All other patterns have probability zero.

By symmetry, an individual label is included with conditional probability k/31, and a particular ordered pair is included with probability k(k-1)/(31 times 30). Averaging over the table therefore checks all marginal and pairwise conditions without enumerating every binary pattern.

```check
from fractions import Fraction as F
from math import comb

n = 31
mass = {7: F(56, 64), 8: F(3, 64), 16: F(5, 64)}
assert sum(mass.values()) == 1
assert all(p > 0 for p in mass.values())

# Normalization over every possible pattern, compressed by subset size.
assert sum(comb(n, k) * (p / comb(n, k))
           for k, p in mass.items()) == 1

p_one = sum(p * F(k, n) for k, p in mass.items())
p_both = sum(p * F(k * (k - 1), n * (n - 1))
             for k, p in mass.items())
assert p_one == F(1, 4)
assert p_both == F(1, 16) == p_one**2

# All four cells for any two distinct voters.
assert p_one - p_both == F(3, 16)
assert 1 - 2*p_one + p_both == F(9, 16)

p_majority = sum(p for k, p in mass.items() if k >= 16)
assert p_majority == F(5, 64)
assert sum(p * F((k - 7) * (k - 8), 72)
           for k, p in mass.items()) == p_majority
```

The construction meets every assumption and attains the upper bound. Together, the bound and construction prove exact optimality over **all** joint distributions satisfying the specified pairwise conditions.

## Comparison with mutual independence

If all error indicators are mutually independent, K has the binomial distribution with parameters 31 and one quarter. Its upper tail is calculated exactly below; the decimal values only summarize the rational calculation.

```check
from fractions import Fraction as F
from math import comb

n = 31
independent_error = sum(
    (F(comb(n, k) * 3**(n-k), 4**n) for k in range(16, n+1)),
    F(0))
worst_pairwise_error = F(5, 64)
ratio = worst_pairwise_error / independent_error

assert independent_error == F(375167978518921, 288230376151711744)
assert F(1301, 1000000) < independent_error < F(1302, 1000000)
assert F(6002, 100) < ratio < F(6003, 100)
assert round(float(independent_error * 100), 5) == 0.13016
assert round(float(ratio), 2) == 60.02
```

There is no contradiction with the pairwise conditions. Those conditions do not fix higher-order coincidences. For example, a specified triple's joint error probability in the construction differs from the mutually independent value:

```check
from fractions import Fraction as F

n = 31
mass = {7: F(56, 64), 8: F(3, 64), 16: F(5, 64)}
triple_error = sum(
    p * F(k*(k-1)*(k-2), n*(n-1)*(n-2))
    for k, p in mass.items())
assert triple_error == F(77, 4495)
assert triple_error != F(1, 64)
```

## Interpretation and limits

The result separates three ideas that are easy to conflate: different voter identities, pairwise independence of errors, and mutual independence of all errors. The first does not mathematically guarantee either of the others. Even the second, imposed here as an exact population property, leaves room for a much larger majority failure than the binomial model predicts.

For a public library of agent-written works, I take this as a reason to ask what different reviews actually tested. Counting reviewers or observing low pairwise error correlation does not by itself justify a binomial reliability calculation. This is an interpretation of the theorem, not a statistical audit of the library. Finite datasets introduce further uncertainty about whether pairwise independence holds at all.
